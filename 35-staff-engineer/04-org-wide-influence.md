# Org-Wide Influence

A Staff Engineer's impact is measured not by the code they write, but by the technical decisions they shape across the organization. This requires navigating organizational dynamics, building alliances, and communicating in ways that resonate with both engineers and executives.

---

## Expanding Your Sphere of Influence

### Map the Organization

Before you can influence the org, you need to understand it:

- **Who makes technical decisions?** Not just titles — who do people actually listen to?
- **Where are the bottlenecks?** Which teams are blocking others? Which systems are slowing everyone down?
- **What are the strategic priorities?** What does leadership care about this quarter and this year?
- **Where is the pain?** Talk to engineers across teams. The problems they complain about are your opportunities.

### Build a Network

Your influence is limited by your relationships. Invest deliberately:

- **Skip-level conversations.** Talk to engineers two levels below the VP. They know where the real problems are.
- **Cross-functional partners.** Build relationships with product managers, designers, and SREs. They see the system from angles you do not.
- **Other Staff+ engineers.** Your peers at the same level are your most important allies. Align with them and your collective influence multiplies.

## Driving Org-Wide Technical Initiatives

### Identifying What Matters

The hardest skill at Staff is choosing what to work on. The organization has hundreds of problems. You need to find the ones where:

1. The impact is high (affects multiple teams or a critical business metric).
2. Nobody else is solving it (if a team already owns it, let them).
3. You are uniquely positioned to help (your skills, context, or relationships give you an advantage).

### Building Consensus

Large technical changes require buy-in from multiple stakeholders. The process:

1. **Socialize privately first.** Before writing the formal proposal, talk to key stakeholders 1:1. Understand their concerns. Incorporate their feedback. By the time you present publicly, the influential voices should already be aligned.
2. **Write the proposal.** An RFC or design doc that frames the problem, explores alternatives, and recommends a path.
3. **Address objections directly.** Do not hope objections go away. Name them in your proposal and explain why you believe the trade-off is worth it.
4. **Set a decision deadline.** Open-ended discussions never converge. "We will decide by Friday. If there are no blocking concerns, we proceed."

### Execution Without Authority

You proposed the migration. Leadership approved it. Now you need six teams to do the work, and none of them report to you.

- **Make it easy.** Provide migration guides, tooling, and examples. The less effort each team needs to spend, the more likely they are to do it.
- **Track progress publicly.** A shared dashboard showing which teams have migrated creates social pressure without you having to nag.
- **Help the stragglers.** When a team falls behind, ask what is blocking them and help remove the obstacle. Do not escalate immediately.
- **Celebrate completion.** When teams finish, acknowledge their work publicly. This builds goodwill for the next initiative.

## Working with Engineering Leadership

Staff Engineers sit at the intersection of technical depth and organizational awareness. Your relationship with engineering leadership (Directors, VPs, CTO) is critical:

- **Be a trusted advisor.** Provide honest technical assessments, especially when the news is bad. Leaders value people who tell them the truth.
- **Translate between layers.** You can explain to leadership why a migration is necessary in business terms, and explain to engineers why a business priority requires technical compromise.
- **Propose, do not wait.** Do not wait for leadership to identify technical problems. Bring them solutions, not just problems.
